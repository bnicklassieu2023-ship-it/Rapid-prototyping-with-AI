# Survey: second test of the Job and the trust condition

**Status:** CLOSED on 13 October 2026, after running from 1 October. Built in Google Forms, 11 questions, about 2 minutes, no sign-in required. **Final result: 21 responses across both forms.** Read against the decision rules in the final section of this file.

**Link sent to respondents:** https://forms.gle/mxvmrjHJAyEPbP3V6

**Where the answers land:** the Responses tab of the form, plus the sheet "Group 3 survey responses" in the team's Google Drive.

**Why we run it:** the 7 filmed interviews give depth on few people. The survey checks whether the same answers hold in a wider group, and it is built so the answers can contradict us, not only confirm us.

## What we cut from the 20-question draft, and why

Country, timing of the last search, the open trigger question, number of interviews, what they did with the last recommendation, which data they would share, who should pay, and willingness to pay. The payment and trigger questions move into the 7 filmed interviews, where follow-up questions are possible. The survey now carries only what can change a decision.

| Finding | Threshold | What we do |

## Limitations we state in the presentation

Convenience sample through our own networks, self-reported behaviour, few respondents outside IE. The survey tests whether our interview findings generalise. It does not prove demand.

## What works against us

**1. The push force did not generalise.** Our opportunity statement rests on students applying at high volume and still not getting hired, which came from interview 01. In round 1, two of four sent 5 applications or fewer, and two of four have been hired through a platform. Only one respondent sits anywhere near the Mass Applier pattern.

The honest reading has two parts and we are not allowed to use only the comfortable one. The charitable part: all four are bachelor students, three aged 21 or under, so they are earlier in the search than interview 01 and the pattern may appear later. The uncomfortable part: we built an opportunity on one student's experience and the first four people we asked do not look like him. Until the interviews say otherwise, "students apply at high volume and are not hired" is an assumption about a **segment we have not defined**, not a description of students in general.

**2. Students tolerate more bad matches than our strategy assumes.** Interview 01's condition for trust was being shown no unsuitable job at all. Nobody in round 1 chose 1 or 2. All four chose 3 to 5 or more than 5. Accuracy is still the most chosen reason to trust, but the cliff is nothing like as steep as we had been designing against. That is a direct contradiction between our two real-user sources, it is recorded rather than averaged away, and it is a question for the seven interviews.

**3. Privacy ranks last but one.** Only one of four picked "my data stays private" as a trust factor. We have just written a privacy position into [privacy.md](privacy.md). Round 1 says that position is a principle we hold, not a demand we discovered, and it should be presented that way rather than as something students asked for.

**4. One respondent asked for the opposite of our Reduce row.** "I would like a platform that centralizes all the job offers in one place." Our ERRC Reduce row reduces the breadth of listings on purpose. See [ERRC.md](ERRC.md) for how that tension is now recorded.

## What the final results change

**1. Accuracy is tied, not ahead.** Round 1's strongest line was that accuracy was the only option all four chose. In the final sample, accuracy, explanation and seeing where the posting came from are tied at 14 of 21, and verified employers is one behind at 13. Accuracy is still in the top group, so the rule we set still fires. But anything in this repository that says students name accuracy most, or rank it above explanation, now rests on round 1 alone. Explanation is as wanted as accuracy, which weakens the interview 01 finding that put accuracy ahead of explanations.

**2. The push force did not generalise, and this is the result that works most against us.** 12 of 21 have been hired through a platform. Only 1 of 21 sent more than 50 applications, and nobody sent more than 100. At most 9 of 21 can be high-volume applicants who were not hired. Round 1 pointed the same way on four people; the final sample confirms the direction on 21. "Students apply at high volume and still do not get hired" describes interview 01, not this sample. The charitable reading from round 1 no longer fits as well, because 10 of 21 are past their undergraduate degree rather than earlier in the search.

**3. Students tolerate bad matches far more than interview 01 did.** Only 1 of 21 chose 1 or 2 bad matches as their limit, nobody chose 1, and 6 of 21 said unsuitable suggestions would not bother them at all. Round 1's finding holds and is stronger. The contradiction with interview 01, who would stop trusting a site after one unsuitable job, now sets one student against twenty.

**4. Coverage is now the most common written objection.** Three of the six written answers ask for completeness: an industry that is underrepresented, wanting to feel "all the available offerings are there", and wanting "a platform that centralizes all the job offers in one place". That puts the challenge to our Reduce row at 3 of 6 written answers, not 1 of 4. ERRC.md says the seven interviews decide it, and that still holds.

**5. Privacy matters more than round 1 suggested.** 9 of 21, against 1 of 4, picked "my data stays private". It is still second from last of the six options, so privacy.md's reading, that this is a position we chose rather than students' main demand, still holds. It is no longer fair to say almost nobody named it.

**6. The university channel holds, and so does the Create row.** 16 of 21 would use it free through their university, and nobody said no. 15 of 21 said the shift view would change what they apply for. Neither reading says anything about whether a careers office would buy the tool or what we can build.

## Final results in one line

21 responses, enough to stop calling the survey a handful of people and still far from a finding. Accuracy is still in the top three but ties with explanation and provenance, students tolerate bad matches far more than interview 01 did, most have already been hired through a platform, and the most common written objection is that a short list misses jobs. All of it goes to the seven interviews, and the push force is now the claim most in need of them.
